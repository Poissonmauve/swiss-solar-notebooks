1. The study in 30 seconds

In 2023, electricity for Swiss households suddenly became much more expensive, because of the European energy crisis of 2022. But the increase was very different from one commune to the next: some communes paid almost nothing more, others paid over 20 centimes more per kilowatt-hour.

We ask: did communes where electricity got more expensive install more solar panels? If yes, it suggests households react to electricity prices, which matters for energy policy.

What we found (so far)

Communes with bigger price increases had faster growth in new solar installations in 2023–24, even when we compare only communes in the same canton. Before the shock, there was no such pattern. But we cannot yet say the price caused it, because the price increases were not random (more in section 11).

The midterm is the exploratory part (EDA = exploratory data analysis): describe the data, show patterns, and list what could make those patterns misleading. The causal analysis comes in the final.

2. Basic words you need

WordMeaning, simply
CommuneA Swiss municipality (Gemeinde). There are 2,110 in 2026. Our unit of observation: one row = one commune.
PV / solar installationA photovoltaic system: solar panels that produce electricity. "Installation" = one system put into service.
kWh, ct/kWhKilowatt-hour is a unit of electricity. A Swiss household uses a few thousand kWh per year. Prices are in centimes (ct) per kWh. +5 ct/kWh on 4,500 kWh ≈ +225 CHF per year.
TariffThe price per kWh the household pays. It has parts: energy (the electricity itself), grid fees (using the network), and taxes/levies.
Grid operator (utility)The local company that delivers electricity and sets the tariff. Switzerland has about 600. A commune is served by one or a few of them. Neighbouring communes can have different operators, so different prices.
Feed-in tariffWhat the operator pays you for the solar electricity you send back to the grid. Also set by the local operator.
ElComThe federal electricity regulator. It publishes every operator's tariffs.
H4 profileElCom's "standard household": a 5-room flat using 4,500 kWh per year. Comparing everyone on the same profile makes tariffs comparable.
CantonOne of the 26 Swiss states. Cantons have their own subsidies and building rules for solar.
Owner-occupierSomeone who lives in a home they own (as opposed to a tenant who rents).

Why a panel pays off through two prices

If you have panels, (1) you buy less electricity, so you save the retail tariff on every kWh you produce and use yourself, and (2) you get the feed-in tariff for what you send to the grid. When the retail tariff jumps, panels pay off faster. That is the mechanism we test.

3. Y, X and Z, and why these

The course asks for three variables: an outcome, a main explanatory variable, and a variable for heterogeneity (does the effect differ between groups?).

Y
Outcome
New solar installations per commune and year.
X
Main explanatory
The 2023 increase in the household tariff (2023 minus 2022), in ct/kWh.
Z
Heterogeneity
Share of dwellings lived in by their owner (census 2000).

Why these choices?

  • Y = number of installations: it directly measures adoption, the decision we care about. It exists for every commune and every year.
  • X = the change, not the level: a commune that always had expensive electricity is different in many ways (rural, small utility…). We look at the shock: how much the price jumped. Combined with looking at growth in Y, this compares each commune with its own past, so permanent differences between communes drop out.
  • X = 2023 minus 2022: 2023 is the year of the big jump. The 2023 tariffs were published in August 2022.
  • Z = owner share: to put panels on a roof you need to own it. Tenants depend on the landlord. So we expect communes with many owners to react more strongly to prices. That is the heterogeneity we test.

Why it is a "causal" question

We want to know if the price increase caused more installations, not just whether both happened. "Did X cause Y?" is causal; "what was the average tariff?" would only be descriptive. The course requires a causal question.

5. How we cleaned it (and why each step)

  1. One commune code for everyone. Swiss communes merge almost every year, and each dataset uses the commune numbers of its own year. We follow the merger register to the commune that exists in 2026. Why: otherwise the same place would appear under different numbers and we could not join the datasets.
  2. Keep only solar plants from the register (337,455 of them).
  3. Place each plant in a commune. 99.3% using their coordinates: we check which commune's shape contains the point. The rest using their postcode. Why: the register doesn't directly give a reliable commune number for every plant.
  4. Build X: for each commune, average the H4 tariff of its operators, then 2023 − 2022.
  5. Build Z: owner-occupied dwellings ÷ all occupied dwellings.
  6. Choose the sample: communes with both a 2022 and a 2023 tariff → 2,098 of 2,110. The 12 dropped have no 2022 tariff (8 in Graubünden). Years 2015–2025 (2026 is incomplete: our register extract is from September 2026).
  7. Count new installations per commune and year → 297,633 plants, 23,078 commune-years (2,098 communes × 11 years).

Sample bias, in one line

Our sample is almost all communes (2,098 of 2,110), so the bias is small. But the 12 dropped are mostly in one canton (Graubünden), and Z is missing for 35 communes that merged before 2010 (the merger register starts in 2010, so we cannot match them).

6. Why logs (and other transformations)

The problem: Y is very skewed

Most communes add a handful of solar systems per year; a few cities add hundreds. In 2023–24 the median commune added 18.5 per year, but the mean is 28.4 and the biggest adds 484. When a few big values pull the mean up like this, we call the variable skewed.

Why raw differences mislead

CommuneBefore (per year)AfterRaw changeRelative change
Village26+4×3 (tripled)
City200260+60×1.3

In raw numbers the city "grew more" (+60 vs +4), only because it is big. Relatively, the village grew much more. We care about how strongly each commune reacted, so we want relative growth.

What the log does

The (natural) logarithm turns ratios into differences: log(after) − log(before) ≈ the percentage change. So a difference in logs measures relative growth, and a village and a city become comparable.

Why log(1 + n) and not log(n)

Some communes had zero installations in a year, and log(0) does not exist. Adding 1 fixes that: log(1 + 0) = 0. Hence our growth measure:

Our Y for the analysis

growth = log(1 + installations per year in 2023–24) − log(1 + installations per year in 2017–21)
Village: log(7) − log(3) = 0.85. City: log(261) − log(201) = 0.26. Now the village correctly shows the bigger reaction.

What a "log point" is

A difference of 0.24 in logs is called 0.24 "log points". To turn it into a percentage: e0.24 − 1 ≈ 0.27, so about 27%. For small values, log points ≈ percent (0.05 ≈ 5%). Because of the "+1", our percentages are approximate, so we say "roughly 27%".

Why 2017–21 and 2023–24, and not 2022?

  • 2017–21 = five normal years before the crisis. Averaging several years smooths out random ups and downs.
  • 2023–24 = the two years after the new tariffs. Two years because the register records the connection date, which comes months after the decision; and installers had waiting lists.
  • 2022 left out: the 2023 tariffs were published in August 2022, so 2022 is half "before", half "after". Including it would blur the comparison.

The other transformations

  • X stays in levels (ct/kWh), no log. It isn't very skewed, and some communes even had a small decrease (minimum −0.5); you can't take the log of a negative number.
  • Outliers in X are kept. 17 communes had increases above 25 ct (up to 43). We checked: they are real, small local utilities, not errors. Deleting real data would bias the result. In the canton chart we only draw the lines up to 25 ct to keep it readable.
  • Z is split at its median (56%) into "below" and "above" for a simple first comparison. In the final we will use it as a continuous variable.

Why each chart type

  • Line chart for things over time (installations per year).
  • Histogram to see the shape of a distribution (is it skewed? where is the middle?). Our Y histogram has its last bar at "95", which means 95 and above.
  • Map to see patterns in space (clusters? neighbours different?).
  • Summary table (slide 7): N (how many), mean, SD (standard deviation: how spread out), min, median, max.

Two key facts about X

  • 74% of the variation is within cantons. If you subtract each canton's average increase from every commune, 74% of the variance is still there. Most of the differences are between communes in the same canton, not between cantons. That's great for us: we can compare within cantons and still have lots of variation.
  • 87% of the increase was the energy part of the price, not grid fees. The crisis was about the cost of electricity itself. (This matters for reverse causality, section 11.)

Mean vs median

The median is the middle value: half the communes are below, half above. The mean is the average. For skewed data, a few huge values pull the mean up, so the median describes the "typical" commune better. That's why we often quote medians.

8. Bivariate analysis: X and Y together

8a. The thirds (slide 12)

We sort communes by X and cut them into three groups of about equal size: low shock (average +2.0 ct), middle (+5.9 ct), high shock (+12.3 ct). Then we follow each group over time.

The index (2021 = 100). The groups have different sizes, so their raw totals aren't comparable. For each group we add up installations per year and divide by that group's 2021 total, ×100. Example: if a group had 1,000 installations in 2021 and 2,900 in 2023, its 2023 index is 290, meaning 2.9 times its 2021 level.

  • Before 2022: the three lines move together (similar trends).
  • 2023: high-shock third 290, middle 265, low-shock 238.
  • 2024: the low-shock communes got their increase a year later (+5.4 ct vs +1.1 for high), so the tariff gap halved (8.6 → 4.3 ct).

Limit of this chart

The index adds up installations, so big communes (cities) weigh much more. That's why we also do the commune-by-commune analysis, where every commune counts once.

8b. Commune by commune, within cantons (slide 13)

Why "within cantons"? Cantons have their own solar subsidies, building rules, and climate. If one canton had big tariff increases and generous subsidies, we'd wrongly credit the tariff. So we only compare communes with others in the same canton.

How: for each commune, subtract its canton's average X from its X, and its canton's average growth from its growth. Now each commune is measured "relative to its canton". Example: in a canton where the average increase is 3 ct, a commune with 8 ct gets +5. Then we fit a straight line through all communes.

(This is exactly the same as a regression with "canton fixed effects", a term you might hear in Q&A.)

The chart: each light dot is a commune; the dark dots are averages of 20 equal-size groups (a "binned scatter": it makes the pattern visible through the cloud); the red line is the fitted line.

The result

Slope = 0.24: within the same canton, +10 ct/kWh of increase goes with 0.24 log points faster growth, roughly 27%. 95% confidence interval: 0.20 to 0.28.
Placebo (same test, before the shock): 0.02, interval −0.03 to 0.07, which is zero.

9. Statistics basics we use

Correlation (r)

A number from −1 to +1 saying how much two variables move together in a straight line. +1: perfectly together; 0: no linear link; −1: perfectly opposite. Our examples: retail increase vs feed-in change r = 0.79 (strong); X vs solar already installed in 2021 r = 0.03 (none); X vs 2022 tariff level r = −0.26 (weak, negative).

Slope

How much Y changes, on average, when X goes up by one unit. We express it per +10 ct.

Confidence interval (CI)

A range of plausible values for the true slope, given the noise in the data. "95% CI 0.20 to 0.28" means: our best guess is 0.24, and values from 0.20 to 0.28 are consistent with the data. Because 0 is not in the interval, the pattern is very unlikely to be pure chance. (That's what "statistically significant" means.)

Clustering by operator

All communes served by the same operator got the same price shock, so they are not 2,098 independent experiments; it's closer to 495 independent shocks (495 main operators). If we ignored that, the CI would be too narrow (too confident). "Clustered by operator" widens the CI to account for it.

Placebo test

A fake test where the answer should be "nothing". We take the same X and test whether it predicts growth before the shock (2017–18 → 2020–21), when nobody knew about 2023. If high-shock communes were already growing faster for other reasons, the placebo would be positive. It is 0.02 (zero), so our pattern is not just an old trend. This is a key check.

Variance

A measure of how spread out values are. "74% of the variance is within cantons" means most of the spread in X is between communes in the same canton.

10. Heterogeneity: adding Z (slide 14)

Heterogeneity = does the relationship differ between types of communes? We split communes at the median owner share (56%) and redo the thirds chart in each half (low vs high shock).

  • Below the median: 2023 index high shock 280 vs low 241.
  • Above the median (more owners): 308 vs 237: a bigger gap, as we expected.

Why it's only "descriptive"

  1. Above the median, the high-shock group already led in 2022 (160 vs 139), before the new tariffs applied. That could be anticipation (tariffs were public from August 2022) or an earlier difference.
  2. Owner share moves with other things: smaller communes (r = −0.62 with population) and more single-family houses (r = 0.41). So "more owners" might really mean "more houses with roofs".
  3. It's a median split, not a test with an interval.

11. Why it is not (yet) causal

Correlation is not causation. To say the price caused more solar, the price increase should be "as good as random", i.e. unrelated to anything else that affects solar. The course asks three questions:

Is there something else? (endogeneity / confounders)

Endogeneity = X is linked to other things that also affect Y, so we can't tell which one did it. A confounder is such an "other thing". Ours:

  • Operators chose the increases. Large operators (main operator in 10+ communes) raised less: 5.8 vs 8.5 ct. Large and small utilities may serve different kinds of communes.
  • Feed-in tariffs moved too (r = 0.79 with X). The same operators raised what they pay for solar power. So maybe people reacted to the feed-in tariff, not the retail price. For now, our 0.24 is the reaction to the whole "price package".
  • Everyone was treated. Every commune lived through the crisis and the media coverage; there is no untouched control group. And low-shock communes caught up in 2024. So we measure a short-run difference between more and less affected communes, not the total effect of the crisis.
  • Local factors we don't observe: communal subsidies, installer waiting lists.

Is it the reverse? (reverse causality)

Could more solar have caused higher tariffs? In theory yes: people with panels buy fewer kWh, so operators must spread grid costs over fewer kWh, raising grid fees. But:

  • 2023 tariffs were fixed in August 2022, before the installations we count.
  • X is unrelated to how much solar a commune already had in 2021 (r = 0.03).
  • 87% of the increase was the energy part, not grid fees.

So reverse causality looks unlikely, though these checks don't fully rule it out.

Can we extrapolate? (external validity)

  • One exceptional shock during a crisis with lots of media attention: normal small price changes might have smaller effects.
  • Commune-level data: we see averages, not individual households. We can't say owners themselves reacted more (that would be the "ecological fallacy").
  • Y counts all plants: 12% are above 30 kW (farms, firms), not only homes.
  • Sample bias: 12 communes dropped; Z missing for 35.

Why the 2025 drop is not explained

Installations fell in 2025. We haven't tested why. Possible reasons: tariffs came down in 2025, feed-in tariffs may have fallen, some 2025 plants may not be registered yet. Don't claim more than that.

Our gap

Every study had one narrow source of price variation. We found no solar study that uses the 2023 Swiss tariff jump: one sudden shock, passed on by hundreds of operators by different amounts, which lets us compare communes within the same canton. Honest caveat: our shock also came with feed-in changes and a crisis everyone saw.

Structural model (for Q&A): a model built from economic theory (how households decide), fitted to data, then used to simulate "what if" policies. Natural experiment: an event outside researchers' control that creates variation, like a disaster or a sudden price change.

13. What we will do for the final

  • Regression within cantons, year by year (an "event study"): compare high- and low-shock communes every year, to see when the gap opens and check there was no gap before (pre-trends) or anticipation in 2022.
  • Neighbouring communes with different operators: compare communes right next to each other, which share almost everything except the operator.
  • Z as a continuous variable, with better ownership data if possible.
  • Feed-in tariffs: try to separate the effect of the retail price from the feed-in price.

The basic idea behind this is difference-in-differences: compare the change over time (before → after) between more and less affected communes. It works if both groups would have followed the same trend without the shock ("parallel trends"). Our placebo is a first check of that.
